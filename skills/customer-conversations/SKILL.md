# Customer conversations: the Mom Test

Based on Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* (2013). People, especially kind ones, will tell you what they think you want to hear. This method gets real information out of conversations anyway.

**Scope.** This skill is for conversations where your goal is to *learn* from the other person. For *explaining* yourself live, use `dynamic-explanation`. For tense or emotional conversations, use `high-stakes-conversations`.

Use it for customer discovery and startup idea validation, but also for: client discovery calls, gathering requirements from stakeholders, user research, scoping what a manager or client actually needs, and checking whether a pitch has real interest behind it.

The name comes from the idea that a good question works even on your mom, who loves you and will lie to protect your feelings. If a question can't be answered with a polite lie, it passes.

---

## The three rules

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Once you describe your idea, people start reacting to it, and protecting your feelings, instead of telling you about themselves.
2. **Ask about specific past events, not opinions or the future.** "When did this last happen? What did you do?" beats "Would you use…?" or "Do you usually…?" People are hopelessly optimistic about what they *will* do.
3. **Talk less, listen more.** The more you're talking, the less you're learning. Pitching is not discovery.

---

## Good questions vs bad questions

**Bad**, because they invite compliments, guesses or polite lies:
- "Do you think this is a good idea?"
- "Would you buy a product that does X?"
- "How much would you pay for X?"
- "Would you ever…?" / "Do you usually…?" / "Might you…?"
- "What would your dream product do?"

**Good**, because they're grounded in facts and behaviour:
- "Talk me through the last time that happened."
- "Why does that bother you? What does it cost you?"
- "What else have you tried? What are you using now?"
- "How are you dealing with it today? What does that cost in time or money?"
- "Have you looked for a solution? What did you find, and why didn't it work?"
- "Where does the money for this come from? Who else is involved in the decision?"
- "Who else should I talk to?"
- "Is there anything else I should have asked?"

Useful truths behind these:
- **Opinions are worthless; past behaviour is data.**
- People know their problems well but are poor at designing solutions. Learn the problem; don't take their proposed fix at face value.
- **Understand their goals first.** Without them you can't judge which problems matter.
- **Some problems don't matter.** If they haven't already tried to solve it, or spent anything on it, it probably isn't painful enough to pay for.
- **Watching beats asking.** If you can, see them do the task.
- **Ask the scary question.** Every conversation should include at least one question you're nervous to ask: about budget, the real decision-maker, legal blockers, or whether they'd actually switch. Skipping it is how you end up surprised later.

## Avoiding bad data: three things that sound like progress

| Bad data | What it sounds like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Compliments** | "That's so cool!" · "I love it." | Deflect and redirect to facts: "Thanks! How are you handling this today?" A compliment often means they're politely ending the conversation. |
| **Fluff**: generalities, hypotheticals, future promises | "I always…" · "I would definitely…" · "I might…" | Anchor it to a real instance: "When was the last time? Can you walk me through it?" |
| **Ideas and feature requests** | "You should add…" | Note it, then dig into why: "What would that let you do? How are you coping without it?" Understand the motivation; don't add it to a roadmap automatically. |

**Dig beneath strong signals.** When someone shows emotion ("This drives me crazy"), asks for something, or states an opinion, ask why. What's behind it? What does it cost them? How often does it happen?

**Read lukewarm signals honestly.** A "meh" or "huh, neat" means they don't care, and that's valuable, reliable information. Don't upgrade your pitch to win them over. Take note and move on.

## Common mistakes

- **Fishing for compliments**: "Do you like my idea?"
- **Exposing your ego**: "I quit my job for this. Be honest!" They'll be gentle instead.
- **Being pitchy**: "No, you're not getting it, it also does…"
- **Being too formal**: interview scripts, rating scales, "Let's set up a meeting." Formality makes people guarded.
- **Being a learning bottleneck**: one person does all the conversations and the team just hears "customers told me so." Share notes and exact quotes.
- **Collecting compliments instead of facts and commitments**: "Everyone loves it!" is not evidence.

## Commitment and advancement

When it's time to test whether the interest is real, push for a next step that costs the other person something. A meeting that ends with everyone feeling good and no next step was a failure. Rejection is better than ambiguity, because it's information.

**What they can give up (from weakest to strongest):**
- **Time**: a follow-up meeting with a clear agenda, feedback on a prototype, using a trial for a meaningful period.
- **Reputation**: introducing you to their boss, colleagues or the decision-maker, or giving a public testimonial or case study.
- **Money**: a letter of intent, a pre-order, a deposit, a paid pilot.

The more they give up, the more seriously you can take what they say. Kind words with no commitment don't count.

- **Zombie leads** are people who stay friendly but never move forward. They clog your pipeline and your judgement. Give each lead a concrete chance to commit or say no.
- **Know the next step before you walk in.** If you don't know what should happen after the meeting, it'll drift.
- **Early on, learning is the real goal.** Early sales are a way of learning.

## Keep it casual

- Many conversations don't need a formal meeting. A five-minute chat in context (at an event, a coffee, while you're both working on the problem) often gets more honest answers.
- If it feels like they're doing you a favour by talking to you, it's too formal, or you haven't shown them what's in it for them.
- Give away as little about your idea as possible while you're learning.

## Finding people and framing the ask

- **Pick a focused segment you can actually find.** "Small businesses" is too broad. Narrow it down: *which* people, and *where* can you find them? If you're hearing mixed signals, you're probably talking to several segments at once.
- Use warm introductions. Almost everyone is a few introductions away from anyone, and every conversation should end with "Who else should I talk to?"
- Create your own opportunities: host an event, write something useful for the group, speak at a meetup, so people come to you.
- **Framing a cold request: Vision → Framing → Weakness → Pedestal → Ask** (memory aid: *"Very Few Wizards Properly Ask"*):
  1. **Vision**: the problem you're trying to solve or the change you want to make (not your product).
  2. **Framing**: what stage you're at, and that you're not selling anything (if true).
  3. **Weakness**: the specific thing you're stuck on, which shows you won't waste their time.
  4. **Pedestal**: why *they* in particular can help.
  5. **Ask**: ask for their help, plainly.

## Before, during and after

**Before**
- Choose a focused, findable segment.
- Agree your **three big learning goals**: the questions you most need answered right now. (Rewrite them as you learn.)
- Decide what commitment or next step you'd ideally get.
- Write down your best guesses about what this person cares about.
- Look up anything you could find through desk research, so the conversation isn't wasted on it.

**During**
- Frame the conversation, keep it casual, and ask questions that pass the Mom Test.
- Deflect compliments, anchor fluff, and dig beneath strong signals.
- Ask your scary question.
- Ask for a commitment or next step, if it's relevant.
- Take notes, ideally with two people: one talking, one writing.

**Notes**
- Capture exact quotes in quotation marks, so you know they're verbatim.
- Tag notes with quick symbols, for example: emotion (excited / angry / embarrassed), **pain**, **goal**, **obstacle**, **workaround**, **background**, plus feature requests, purchasing process and money. A pain said with real emotion counts for more than one said in passing.
- Keep notes somewhere the whole team can search.

**After**
- Review with the team: key quotes, what you learned, and how it changes your beliefs and your big three questions.
- Review the conversation itself: which questions worked, and which signals did you miss?
- Keep going until you stop hearing new things.

---

## How to apply this skill

- **Preparing someone for conversations:** produce a prep sheet: the segment, the big three questions, a list of questions that pass the Mom Test (plus the scary one), the target commitment, and a framing message using Vision → Framing → Weakness → Pedestal → Ask if outreach is needed.
- **Reviewing a script, survey or outreach message:** mark every question that fails the test (opinion, hypothetical, future, leading, pitch) and rewrite it as a question about specific past behaviour.
- **Interpreting notes or transcripts:** separate facts and commitments from compliments, fluff and ideas. Summarise pains, goals, obstacles, workarounds and money signals with quotes, flag zombie leads, and give an honest verdict: is there real evidence of a problem worth solving?
- **Role-play:** play the customer, give realistic polite lies and fluff, and coach the user to deflect, anchor and dig in.
