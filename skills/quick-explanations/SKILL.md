# Quick explanations: short conversations, emails and messages

Based on Ros Atkins' *The Art of Explanation* (2023). Most everyday explaining happens in short calls, meetings, emails and messages, where the full Seven Steps would be overkill. Good habits do the work here. It applies to work and practical communication, not chats with friends and family.

For in-depth pieces use `explain-clearly`. For interviews and Q&As use `dynamic-explanation`.

---

## Quick verbal explanations (calls, short meetings, appointments)

Before any non-social conversation, take one to five minutes and jot down answers to three questions:
1. **Which subjects do I want to cover?** Usually a short list, but make it complete.
2. **What do I want to say on each?** A bullet or two of the points or facts you'd hate to forget.
3. **What do I want to ask?** The questions you must get answered.

Keep the note in view while you talk. Before the conversation ends, check it and pick up anything missed ("Just before we finish…"). If you'd rather not bring notes, cut each subject to one trigger word.

## Short written explanations (emails, messages, notices)

### Five working assumptions
These aren't cynical, just realistic in a crowded inbox:
1. It might not be read at all.
2. It might not be read to the end.
3. It will be skimmed, not read line by line.
4. The reader wants information, not a pleasant read.
5. If it doesn't feel meant for them personally, they're much less likely to read or reply.

### What to do about each

**1 → Get it opened and started**
- **Subject line**: make it clear this is for this person and about something that matters to them. Their name, or the specific thing they care about, works better than a vague topic.
- **First sentence says what the message is**: Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF). Many apps show only the first line or two.
  - "Hi Maya, three questions on the launch, and I need answers by Thursday."
  - "Hi Maya, here's everything you need for next week's offsite."
- Skip the warm-up opener ("Hope you're well…"). Exception: if you don't know the person, or the relationship calls for warmth, use one short courteous line, then get to the point.

**2 → Get it read to the end**
- **Make it as short as it can be.** Cutting words reliably raises read and reply rates, and is worth the extra effort.

**3 → Make it skimmable**
- Short paragraphs, one idea each, with space around them.
- Headers, or bold lead-ins, that say what each section holds.
- Use formatting sparingly. Every highlight draws the eye away from everything else, so too much emphasis makes the rest less likely to be read.
- **Fragments are fine.** Logistics often read best as a list:
  ```
  When: Sat 14 June, 10am–12pm
  Where: Riverside Community Hall, Mill Lane
  Bring: water bottle, trainers
  Cost: free
  ```
- **FAQ format**: write the questions the reader will have as headings and answer each in one line.
- Put important actions in their own space. If thanks or pleasantries would sit in front of the action, move them to the end.

**4 → Treat it as an information exchange**
- Be explicit about what you need, from whom, and by when.
- **Number your questions**, keep each self-contained, and give any context in the question itself, so the reader can reply with a word under each.
  ```
  Hi Sam, two quick questions so I can send the contract today.
  1. Legal approved the draft unchanged. OK for me to send without you reviewing again?
  2. Do you want to join the client call Friday at 3pm? It's only covering timelines.
  ```
- Include **all the details the reader will need**: date, time, place, cost, deadline, contact. Every missing detail creates follow-up emails and confusion.

**5 → Make it personal**
- Avoid group emails where you can. Individual messages get more replies.
- If you have to write to a group, make it easy for each person to find their own part. Use sections by role or by name ("**Speakers:** please send slides by noon the day before").
- Make sure each person can tell what's being asked of them.

### Pre-send checklist
- [ ] Does the subject line show who it's for and why it matters to them?
- [ ] Does the first sentence say what this is and what I need?
- [ ] Is it as short as it can be? Can any sentence or paragraph go?
- [ ] Can someone skimming find everything in five seconds?
- [ ] Are the asks numbered and explicit, with deadlines?
- [ ] Are all the logistics included?
- [ ] Are pleasantries out of the way of the action?
- [ ] Is any jargon, acronym or reference the reader might not know explained or removed?
- [ ] Is formatting used sparingly?

## How to apply this skill

- **Drafting**: write the message ready to send. Put the subject line first, then BLUF, then skimmable sections, then numbered asks. Ask about the purpose or recipient only if the draft can't be written without it.
- **Improving the user's draft**: return the rewritten version, then a few one-line notes on the main changes (e.g. "moved the ask to the first line", "cut 60% of words", "split three questions into a numbered list").
- **Before a call or meeting**: produce the three-question prep note (subjects · points · questions) as a compact list the user can glance at.
