# Dynamic explanation: preparing for situations you can't script

Based on Ros Atkins' *The Art of Explanation* (2023). Use it when someone has to explain things live, where others pick the questions and the order: job interviews, media interviews, panels, Q&As, pitch meetings, performance reviews, medical appointments, difficult conversations.

You can't control what you're asked, but you can control how ready you are. The aim is to make most of the decisions in advance (what to include, how pieces link, how to phrase the hard parts) so that in the moment your mind is free to listen and respond.

For scripted pieces use `explain-clearly`. For emails and quick calls use `quick-explanations`.

---

## Steps 1–3: Prepare the information
These are the same as the prepared method:
- **Set-up**: write the purpose in one sentence. Then: who you'll be talking to, what they know, what they want from you, the format, and how long you'll have.
- **Find**: gather everything relevant, from sources you can trust, and keep track of where it came from. Keep lists of likely questions and of things you don't fully understand yet, and close those gaps.
- **Distil**: cut every item down to its essential nugget. Keep only what serves the purpose.

## Step 4: Organise into strands
Notes for live use need to be lighter than notes for a script.
- Divide the material into **strands**, one per distinct area of the subject.
- Give each strand the same shape and **keep it to about five elements**. More than that is hard to hold in your head.
  ```
  STRAND: <short header>
  Main point: <the one thing this strand exists to say>
  Fact 1
  Fact 2
  Fact 3
  Context: <why it matters / how it connects>
  ```
  *Example (job interview):* Main point: I can run large, complex events · Ran a 1,500-person summit · Owned budget, staffing and marketing · Delivered events in four countries · Promoted to lead the events team · Context: matches the scale this role needs.
- Know the single purpose of each strand. If you said it out loud, what would it achieve?
- You don't need to write out everything. Each line is a prompt you can expand on if there's time.
- If you have many strands, **group** the ones that belong together.

## Step 5: Verbalise (the step that makes the difference)
Notes you've only read are much harder to use under pressure than material you've already said out loud. Practise saying it:
1. Talk through each strand out loud, several times, until the phrasing feels natural.
2. Practise **moving between strands in different orders**, because live questions won't follow your order. Use bridging phrases such as:
   - "That's one side of it. The other is…"
   - "And while that matters, so does…"
   - "To really understand this, we also need to look at…"
   - "If X is one part of the answer, Y is another."
   - "There's more than one way to see this. Another is…"
3. Keep going until you can get from any strand to any other smoothly.

## Step 6: Memorise (selectively)
- Decide what really has to be memorised. Usually that's the opening, key figures, a few precise phrases for sensitive points, and a closing line. Ask why you'd need to memorise anything more.
- Cut each strand down to **trigger words**: the fewest words that bring the whole strand back. Chunk related items together.
- Practise without notes: each strand, then the whole thing.

## Step 7: Questions
**Predict them.** People and topics are fairly predictable. Build a list:
1. What questions are you very likely to get?
2. If you were asking, and curious, what would *you* want to know?
3. If you wanted to be awkward, what would you ask?
4. Which questions would you rather not get, perhaps because you haven't worked out how to answer them yet?
5. What might come up from the edges of the subject?
6. What can you find out about the people asking? (Past interviews, colleagues' advice, how the organisation sees the issue.)

**Sort them into three groups and work each one:**
- *Can answer*: merge duplicates. For each, decide which strands you'd use and answer it out loud. Length doesn't matter yet; sometimes "yes" is the whole answer.
- *Need to work on the answer*: don't avoid these. Find the strands that help and work out the phrase that pulls them together. Practise out loud. If it won't come, take a break and come back to it.
- *Need new information*: get just enough to say something reasonable, then check you can.

**In the moment:**
- Use the time while the question is being asked to start choosing your strands.
- Draw on the strands you've prepared; that work is already done.
- Use bridging phrases to move between strands in whatever order the answer needs.
- **Reuse the question's own words** in your answer, so it obviously answers what was asked.
- **If you don't know**: say so plainly, give the nearest thing you do know, and offer to follow up. Don't bluff.
- Let your preparation give you confidence. If you've done this work, you're ready.

## How to apply this skill

When helping the user prepare, produce a **prep pack**:
1. A one-line purpose and a short audience note, including what they know, what they want, and any credibility gap to close.
2. **Strand cards** in the format above, grouped if there are many.
3. A short bank of **bridging phrases** written in the user's own voice.
4. A **question list** sorted into the three groups, with a sketch of each answer (which strands, plus any key phrase) and particular care on the awkward questions.
5. A **trigger-word sheet** for memorising, and the lines worth knowing exactly.
6. A **rehearsal plan**: strands aloud, then transitions in random order, then a full run-through without notes.

Then offer a **live mock**: play the interviewer or audience, ask questions from the list mixed with surprises, and after each answer give brief feedback. Was it the simplest way to say it? Did it answer the question? Did it reuse the question's words? Was there a stronger strand to use?
