# Write well: clear, lean, human prose

Based on William Zinsser's *On Writing Well: The Classic Guide to Writing Nonfiction* (6th ed., 2001). His premise: writing is thinking made visible. Anyone who can think clearly can write clearly, about any subject. Good nonfiction has four qualities: clarity, simplicity, brevity and humanity. Use this skill for any prose a person has to read, including your own replies.

**Scope.** This skill owns the *prose itself*: word choice, sentences, paragraphs, voice and the line-by-line rewrite. Other skills handle the rest:
- `pyramid-principle`: the logic and order of an argument or recommendation.
- `explain-clearly`: audience, research, choosing the material and story, and delivery for in-depth explanations. This skill supplies the sentence-level editing for its tightening step.
- `quick-explanations`: subject lines, length and layout of short emails and messages.
- `high-stakes-conversations`: when the hard part is the emotion.

## 1. Know what you think before you write

Fuzzy prose almost always comes from a fuzzy idea, not from a bad choice of word. Fix the thinking first.

- **Before drafting**, ask: what am I trying to say? Often the honest answer is "I'm not sure yet." Work that out first.
- **After drafting**, ask: have I said it? Would it be clear to someone meeting the subject for the first time?
- **If the reader gets lost, it's the writer's fault.** Readers try for a while, rereading and guessing, and then they give up.
- **Leave one idea behind.** Decide on the single point the reader should take away. One, not several. It sets the route, the tone and the stopping point.
- **Cover a small piece well.** Nobody can write about a whole large subject. Pick the corner you can handle, cover it properly, and stop.
- **Ask what the piece is *really* about.** Material that isn't central goes, however hard it was to gather. What you leave out still shows: the reader can sense you know more than you wrote.

## 2. Cut the clutter

Clutter is any word that does no work. Common forms:
- a long word where a short one would do
- an adverb that repeats its verb
- a passive construction that hides who acted
- a sentence that restates the one before it
- a phrase that announces what you're about to say instead of saying it

Most first drafts can lose a third to a half of their words with no loss of meaning or personality.

- **The bracket test.** Mentally bracket every word, phrase and sentence that isn't pulling its weight. Read the passage without them. If it still works, delete them.
- **Try deletion first.** When a sentence won't come right, writers shuffle the stuck phrase around and add words to prop it up. Ask first whether it needs to be there at all. Usually it doesn't, and removing it frees the sentence.
- **Don't announce. Do it.** "It's worth pointing out that X" becomes "X". "It's interesting that X" means you should make X interesting.
- **Swap inflated phrases for plain ones:**

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| at this point in time, currently | now |
| due to the fact that, in light of the fact that | because |
| in order to, for the purpose of | to, for |
| until such time as | until |
| is able to, has the capacity to | can |
| a majority of | most |
| utilize, facilitate, implement | use, help, do |
| numerous, sufficient, prior to, subsequent to | many, enough, before, after |
| is experiencing an outage | is down |
| free up, head up, meet with | free, head, meet |

## 3. Stop hedging

Small qualifiers dilute prose and spend the reader's trust: *a bit, a little, sort of, kind of, rather, quite, very, fairly, pretty much, somewhat, in a sense, arguably, essentially, basically*. Writing that keeps flinching is hard to believe. State the claim.

- **Don't put a label on a fact before the reader sees it.** *Surprisingly*, *interestingly*, *of course* and *obviously* tell readers how to react. Give them the fact and let them react.
- **Watch for stacked hedges.** "I'd say it may perhaps be somewhat risky" carries four hedges for one claim.
- **Hedge real uncertainty once, in the place it applies, and make it specific.** "I haven't tested this on Windows" is useful information. A blanket softening of every sentence is noise. Calibration and confidence aren't in conflict: be exact about what you know and what you don't, then speak plainly.

## 4. Choose plain, precise words

- **Verbs carry the sentence.** Prefer active verbs: "The script deletes the cache" beats "The cache is deleted by the script." The passive hides the actor, adds words and drains energy. Choose the exact verb. Was the build *slow*, did it *time out*, or did it *crash*?
- **Concrete nouns come next.** Short words for things people can picture hold up over time. Long abstract nouns, often ending in *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ity*, put readers to sleep.
- **Most adverbs and adjectives are redundant.** "Completely eliminate," "quickly rush," "new innovation": the main word already says it. Keep a modifier only when it adds something the noun or verb can't, such as "the *failing* test," when there are many tests.
- **Turn concept nouns back into people doing things.** "There was widespread resistance to the migration" becomes "Most teams refused to migrate." A sentence with no person and no real verb (only *is*) is usually dead.
- **Break noun chains.** "User onboarding flow optimization initiative" is four nouns in a row. Rewrite it as a clause with a verb: "the project to make sign-up easier."
- **Judge new words by need.** Accept a new term if it names something precisely and has no plain equivalent. Reject it if it only sounds important: *leverage* for *use*, *ideate* for *think of*, *impact* as a verb, *learnings* for *lessons*. Be open to new vocabulary and strict about grammar.
- **Avoid stock phrases.** *Game-changer, deep dive, perfect storm, at the end of the day* tell the reader nobody was choosing words.
- **Get the distinctions right:** *fewer/less, imply/infer, affect/effect, comprise* (the whole comprises the parts), *disinterested* (impartial, not bored).
- **Don't write what you wouldn't say.** If a word would sound stiff out loud (*moreover, thus, whilst, individuals*), it sounds stiff on the page.

## 5. Make sentences that move

- **One idea per sentence.** Readers take in ideas one at a time, in order. When a sentence sprawls, it is usually carrying two thoughts. Split it. Most writers should reach the period sooner.
- **Read it aloud,** or hear it in your head. Prose has rhythm even on a screen. If every sentence is the same length and shape, vary them. A short sentence after long ones lands hard.
- **Put the key word last.** The end of a sentence is where emphasis falls and what lingers.
- **Keep the reader oriented.** Each sentence should start from where the previous one left the reader. A pronoun or modifier that could attach to the wrong noun from the last sentence will mislead, even if only for a second.
- **Signal a turn at the start.** *But, yet, still, instead, so, meanwhile, later, now*: put them first so the reader is ready for the change. *But* is a fine way to start a sentence. *However* works better a few words in than at the very start. Mark every jump in time or place.
- **Punctuation:**
  - *Period:* the most useful mark. Use more.
  - *Exclamation point:* almost never. Use word order for emphasis, and never use one to point out that something is funny.
  - *Semicolon:* sparingly; it slows the pace.
  - *Dash:* adds an explanation or an aside. One here and there is useful; one per sentence is a tic.
  - *Colon:* introduces a list or a payoff.
- **Use contractions** where they sound natural. They make prose warmer. Avoid ambiguous ones (*I'd* can mean *I had* or *I would*) and invented ones (*should've* in formal prose).
- **Use *that*** for clauses that pick out which thing you mean. Use ***which***, with a comma, for clauses that add information about something already identified.

## 6. Hold the piece together

- **Paragraphs are units of thought.** Short paragraphs look inviting and give the reader air. A string of one-sentence paragraphs breaks the reasoning apart and feels like being talked down to. Give each paragraph one job and let it finish that job.
- **Unity.** Pick one point of view (*I*, *we*, *you* or third person), one main tense and one tone, and keep them. Switching between a friendly guide and an impersonal manual is jarring.
- **Settle these before starting:** what role you're writing in (expert, guide, colleague), how much ground to cover, and the one point.
- **Follow a better direction if the draft finds one.** Then go back and rewrite the opening so the whole piece agrees with itself.
- **Write in sequence.** Prose is linear, and logic is what joins it. After each sentence, ask what the reader now wants to know, and answer that next.
- **Mark changes of phase** with a heading, a rule or a blank line, so the reader sees how the piece is organised.
- **Prefer paragraphs to bullets for reasoning.** Bullets suit material people scan: steps, options, reference items. An argument or explanation needs the connecting words (*because, so, but, which means*) that bullets cut out. Don't break a short answer into bullet points or headers.

## 7. Open strong, stop on time

- **The first sentence decides whether the second gets read.** It has to catch the reader's interest and do real work: show what this is and why it's worth reading. Get to the substance quickly.
- **Skip stock openings:** scene-setting that delays the point, a rhetorical question nobody asked, a definition from the dictionary.
- **A story is the oldest way to hold attention.** If information can be delivered as something that happened, consider doing it that way.
- **Make the last sentence of a paragraph count.** It's the bridge to the next one.
- **When you've made your point, stop.** Don't finish with a summary of what you just said. That is a school habit, and readers can feel the restatement grinding. A good ending is a little sooner or sharper than the reader expected and feels right. Good endings include a telling final detail, a quotation, or an echo of the opening.

## 8. Explaining technical material

- **Assume the reader knows nothing** about this specific thing. Don't rely on knowledge you take for granted, or on their memory of an earlier explanation.
- **Explaining a process step by step tests you, then the reader.** It shows whether you really understand it. Then it takes the reader through the same steps that made it click for you. Every step must follow from the last; no leaps.
- **Build an inverted pyramid.** Start with the one fact everything else depends on. Widen with each sentence: how it works, why it matters, what follows from it.
- **Tie the abstract to something familiar.** An analogy or a concrete example the reader can picture does more than a precise definition alone.
- **Define a term when you first use it,** or don't use it.
- **Keep the people in it.** Technical writing reads better when someone is visibly doing something: the user clicks, the server rejects, the team decided.

## 9. Writing for work

- **Institutions don't have to sound like institutions.** People connect with people, not with "operational efficiencies" or "stakeholder alignment." Bring back the missing *I* or *we*: say who will do what.
- **A plain style is not a simple mind.** It takes more thinking than an inflated one. Jargon often hides a writer who hasn't worked out what they mean.
- **Example rewrite:** "Remediation activities were initiated following identification of the issue" becomes "We found the bug on Monday and fixed it Tuesday."
- **Commit messages, pull requests, docs, tickets and status updates are writing.** Say what changed and why, in plain words, with an actor and a verb.

## 10. Voice and attitude

- **Sound like yourself.** Style can't be bolted on; added ornament reads like a costume. Self-conscious drafts often start stiff and come alive a few paragraphs in. Cut the stiff part.
- **Keep the same voice across subjects.** Whether the topic is a database index or a bereavement, it's the same writer: plain, warm and direct. Don't switch to a "fun" register for beginners or a grand one for serious topics.
- **Be careful with craft and relaxed about approval.** Work hard not to lose the reader. Don't twist yourself trying to make every reader like you.
- **Respect the reader.** Avoid matey filler (*you see*, *let's be honest*, winks and jokes signposted as jokes). Being friendly doesn't require sloppy English. The writing that reads most easily has the most careful grammar underneath.
- **Taste is mostly omission.** Leave out clichés, cute synonyms, hype and filler intensifiers.
- **Understate.** Exaggeration tires readers, and one inflated claim makes every later claim suspect.
- **Trust the material.** A strong fact doesn't need "remarkably" in front of it or a sentence after it explaining why it's remarkable. Let the reader do some of the work.
- **Interest shows.** A writer engaged with the subject produces prose people want to keep reading.

## 11. Rewrite

The first draft is raw material. Almost no sentence is right the first time: it's unclear, wordy, flat, or doesn't follow from the one before. Most of the work of writing well is rewriting. That doesn't mean drafting new versions. It means tightening, reordering and sharpening what's there until the piece runs with no wasted parts. Small choices are worth real time: one exact word can carry a sentence.

## Claude's habits to watch

These are the forms clutter takes in AI writing. Check for them every time.

- **Openers that stall:** praising the question, restating it, or announcing what's coming ("Let me break this down"). Start with the answer.
- **Throat-clearing:** *It's worth noting that, It's important to remember that, Keep in mind that.* Delete the frame and keep the fact.
- **The recap ending:** *In summary, Overall, To sum up*, followed by what the reader just read. End when the content ends.
- **Stock sign-offs:** *Let me know if you have any other questions!* or *Hope this helps!*
- **Triplets of near-synonyms:** "clear, concise, and readable"; "robust, scalable, and reliable." Pick the one that's true.
- **Favourite filler words:** *delve, leverage, utilize, robust, seamless, streamline, comprehensive, crucial, ensure, landscape, realm, navigate, foster, tapestry.* Each usually stands in for a plainer word, or for nothing.
- **Reflexive hedging** on claims you're sure of, and blanket caveats unrelated to anything specific.
- **Formatting as decoration:** headers on a four-sentence answer, bullets in place of reasoning, bold scattered across a paragraph.
- **Exclamation points and em dashes** used as default punctuation.
- **Telling the reader how to feel:** *Excitingly, Importantly, Interestingly.*

## How to apply this skill

**When writing for the user:**
- Settle the one point first, then write it plainly. Lead with the answer.
- Before sending, run the revision checklist below in your head and fix what fails. Deliver the clean version. Don't show your edits.
- Match length to need. Short questions get short answers.

**When editing the user's draft:**
- Keep their voice and meaning. Your job is to remove what gets in the way, not to replace their style with yours.
- Return the revised text first, then a few one-line notes on the main changes ("cut the opening paragraph; the piece starts at paragraph two", "turned five passives active").
- Show before → after on a few representative sentences when teaching, not general advice.
- Flag anything you cut that changes meaning, so they can restore it.

## Revision checklist

1. **Point:** Can I state the one point in a sentence? Does the opening deliver it, or get to it fast?
2. **Clutter:** What fails the bracket test? Is there repetition, an announcement, or a recap ending?
3. **Hedges:** Is each remaining qualifier marking real, specific uncertainty?
4. **Verbs and nouns:** Are passives active where possible, and abstract nouns turned into people doing things?
5. **Words:** Is there a plain word for every inflated one? Any stock phrases or filler words?
6. **Flow:** Does each sentence pick up where the last left off? Are turns signalled at the start?
7. **Sound:** Do sentence lengths vary? Does each sentence end on its key word?
8. **Format:** Are bullets and headers used only where the reader will scan? Any stray exclamation points or em dashes?
9. **Ending:** Does it stop when the content stops?
