# The Pyramid Principle: answer first, logic underneath

Based on Barbara Minto's *The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving* (3rd ed., 2010). It is the standard way consulting firms and banks structure written and spoken communication. Use it for memos, emails to senior people, decks and slide titles, investment or client recommendations, status updates, and for structuring an analysis before any work starts.

Pairs with `explain-clearly` (overall craft of explanation) and `quick-explanations` (email mechanics).

---

## The core idea

Readers take in ideas one at a time and automatically try to group them. If you don't tell them up front what the ideas add up to, they'll guess, and often guess wrong. So:

> **Give the summarizing idea first, then the ideas it summarizes.**

Arrange every document as a pyramid: one governing thought at the top, a few key points under it, and supporting points under each of those.

```
                    [ Governing thought / answer ]
                   /             |               \
          [Key point 1]    [Key point 2]    [Key point 3]      ← the "key line"
           /    \            /    \            /    \
        support support   support support   support support
```

### Three rules for every pyramid
1. **Ideas at any level summarize the ideas grouped below them.**
2. **Ideas in each group are the same kind of idea**, so you can name them with one plural noun (reasons, steps, problems, risks, recommendations).
3. **Ideas in each group are in a logical order**, and you can say which order you used (see "Ordering" below).

Keep groups small, roughly three to five items, because people can't hold more than a handful of ideas at once.

---

## The two relationships

### Vertical: a question-and-answer dialogue
Each statement raises a question in the reader's mind (*Why? How? Which ones? So what?*). The points directly below it answer that question, and nothing else.
- Top: "We should exit the Midwest distribution business." → Reader asks: *Why?*
- Below: three reasons. Each reason raises its own *Why?* or *How do you know?*, answered one level further down.

Test it: read any box, ask the question it raises, and check that the boxes below answer exactly that.

### Horizontal: deductive or inductive
Points in a group relate to each other in one of two ways:
- **Inductive** (preferred for the key line): parallel ideas that are all the same kind and together support the point above. ("Three reasons to exit: margins are falling, volume is shrinking, and the capital can earn more elsewhere.")
- **Deductive**: a chain of reasoning: a statement about the world, a related statement (comment on the subject or predicate of the first), and the conclusion that follows. ("Our clients are consolidating vendors → we're their fourth-largest vendor → we're at risk of being cut, so we need to move up their list.")
  - Use deduction sparingly and keep chains to about four steps. It forces the reader through every step before reaching the point. Higher in the pyramid, prefer induction.

## The introduction: Situation → Complication → Question → Answer

The introduction tells the reader something they already know, then something that changes it, so the question your document answers arises naturally in their mind, and you answer it immediately.

| Element | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | An uncontroversial statement the reader already agrees with. | "We've been the client's lead bank on its last three financings." |
| **Complication** | What happened that creates tension or a problem in that situation. | "Their new CFO has invited four banks to pitch for the upcoming refinancing." |
| **Question** | The question the complication raises (often left implicit). | "How do we keep the mandate?" |
| **Answer** | Your governing thought, the top of the pyramid. | "We should lead with a pre-emptive structure that reduces their interest cost and show our execution record, in a meeting before the formal pitch." |

Guidelines:
- Keep the introduction short. It reminds; it doesn't inform. Put only what the reader will agree with, and no new arguments.
- The Situation-Complication-Question-Answer order is the default. For a busy senior reader you can lead with the **Answer** and fold Situation and Complication in briefly afterward. Answer-first is the norm for executive emails.
- **Common patterns:**
  - *Giving direction*: situation (we need to do X), complication (here's how), question (how?), answer (the steps).
  - *Seeking approval to spend money*: problem, proposed solution, cost, payback.
  - *Choosing among alternatives*: what we need to decide, the criteria, the alternatives against them, the recommendation.
  - *Progress review*: what we set out to do, where we are, what's next and any decisions needed.

## Ordering the ideas in a group

Every inductive group must follow one of three orders, and only three:

| Order | Use when the ideas are… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Time (sequence)** | Steps, causes and effects, a process | "First rebuild the model, then refresh the comps, then update the deck." |
| **Structural** | Parts of a whole (an org, a geography, a process map, a balance sheet) | "North America, Europe, Asia" or "Revenue, costs, capital" |
| **Degree (ranking)** | Items sharing a characteristic, ranked by how much | "The three biggest risks, largest first" |

If you can't tell which order a group follows, the grouping is probably wrong. Re-check that the ideas really are the same kind.

**MECE**: groups should be **mutually exclusive** (no overlaps) and **collectively exhaustive** (nothing missing at that level). Overlaps and gaps are signs the logic is muddled.

---

## Summarize, don't label

The summary point above a group must say what the group *means*. It should not be an intellectually blank label.
- ❌ "There are three issues with the proposal." / "Key findings" / "Next steps"
- ✅ "The proposal underprices risk in all three segments it targets."

Two cases:
- **Grouped actions** (steps, recommendations): the summary is the **result** of doing them. Make each action specific about the outcome it produces ("Cut close time from 10 days to 5"), not vague ("Improve the close process"). Check that every action is at the same level, and that sub-steps aren't mixed in with the main ones.
- **Grouped situations or findings**: the summary is the **insight** they share. Look for what the points have in common, then make the inductive leap to what it implies. ("All three regions lost share to the same competitor on price → our pricing, not our product, is the problem.")

If you can't write a non-blank summary, you haven't finished thinking.

## Building the pyramid

### Top-down (when you roughly know your answer)
1. Write the **subject**.
2. Write the **question** in the reader's mind about that subject.
3. Write your **answer**: the governing thought.
4. Write the **situation** (what the reader already accepts about the subject).
5. Write the **complication** that triggers the question. Check that situation and complication really lead to the question and answer.
6. Ask the **question the answer raises** (why / how / which), and answer it with the key line points.
7. Repeat for each key line point, down to the level of supporting detail.

### Bottom-up (when you only have a pile of points)
1. **List** all the points you want to make.
2. **Work out the relationships**: group points that are the same kind; spot cause and effect; turn each group into a summarizing point.
3. **Draw conclusions** from the groups, then ask what they add up to. That's your governing thought. Then write the introduction.

Most real work moves between the two.

## Problem solving with the pyramid

Use the same logic before you write, when scoping an analysis:
- **Define the problem:** opening scene (the setting) → disturbing event (what changed) → **R1**, the undesired result (where we are now) → **R2**, the desired result (where we want to be). The question is how to get from R1 to R2, or why we're at R1.
- **Structure the analysis:** use a diagnostic framework that's MECE, such as how a process physically works, a cause-and-effect chain, or a classification of possible causes. Then use a **logic tree** to break the solution space into MECE options, or an **issue tree** of yes/no questions that each have an analysis behind them.
- Don't start crunching data before you know which question each piece of analysis answers.

## Presenting the pyramid

- **Documents:** headings that mirror the pyramid levels. Every heading is a full summarizing statement, not a label. Use parallel phrasing across headings at the same level. Every section opens with its point.
- **Decks:** each slide has an **action title** that states its conclusion in one full sentence ("EMEA margin fell 300bp, almost all of it from freight"). Read only the titles in order and they should tell the whole story as a pyramid. The body of the slide proves the title and nothing else.
- **Executive summary / storyline page:** governing thought + key line, each a full sentence.
- **Transitions:** use them to show where the reader is in the structure (look back at what was just covered, look ahead to the next key point), and keep them short.
- **Emails:** the answer or ask in the first line, then the key line as a short numbered or bulleted list, then support only as needed.

---

## Review checklist

- [ ] Is there one governing thought, stated as a full sentence that answers the reader's question?
- [ ] Does the introduction contain only things the reader already accepts, and does the complication lead clearly to the question?
- [ ] Does each level answer the question raised by the level above it?
- [ ] Is each group the same kind of idea, MECE, and three to five items?
- [ ] Does each group follow a stated order (time, structure or degree)?
- [ ] Is every summary an insight or result, and never a blank label like "key findings"?
- [ ] Are the headings or slide titles full sentences that tell the story when read alone?
- [ ] Is deduction kept short and kept low in the pyramid?

---

## How to apply this skill

- **Drafting:** sketch the pyramid first (governing thought, key line, supports) as a compact outline. If the user gave no answer, ask for it or propose one. Then write the document or email from that outline.
- **Reviewing the user's draft:** reverse-engineer its pyramid. Show what the governing thought currently is (or that there isn't one), flag groups that mix kinds of ideas, aren't MECE or have no order, and flag blank summaries. Then return a restructured outline and a rewritten version, answer first.
- **Decks:** produce the storyline as a list of action titles. Check that it reads as an argument before building the slides.
- **Analysis scoping:** write R1/R2 and the question, then a MECE issue tree with the analysis needed for each branch.
