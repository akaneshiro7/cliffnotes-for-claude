# Explain clearly: the Seven-Step method

A method for building prepared explanations, based on Ros Atkins' *The Art of Explanation* (2023). Use it for any explanation where there is time to prepare and the explainer controls the words: essays, reports, briefings, presentations, speeches, scripts, pitches, long explainers, documentation of a concept.

**Scope.** This skill owns the craft of explaining: the audience, finding and distilling the material, plain language, story, tightening and delivery. Other skills handle the rest:
- `pyramid-principle`: the logical structure when the piece argues for a decision or recommendation (a memo, analysis or deck storyline). In that case, use pyramid structure in place of the story structures in Step 4, and use this skill for everything else.
- `dynamic-explanation`: unscripted situations like interviews, meetings and Q&A.
- `quick-explanations`: emails, messages and quick calls.

**Match the effort to the job.** A three-paragraph explainer in chat gets the steps run quickly in your head. A keynote or a long report gets every step done explicitly. Either way, the ten tests below always apply.

---

## The ten tests of a good explanation

Keep these questions in mind the whole way through, and run them all as a final check.

| # | Quality | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Simplicity | Is this the simplest way to say it? Long words, long sentences, extra adjectives and stray detail all get in the way of understanding. Simple doesn't mean short. |
| 2 | Essential detail | What detail does this explanation actually need? Detail is the point of explaining, but each non-essential fact makes the essential ones harder to see. |
| 3 | Complexity | Is there any part of this I don't understand myself? You can't explain what you don't understand. Wanting to skip a hard part is a sign you aren't ready to explain it yet. |
| 4 | Efficiency | Is this the most succinct way to say it? Give the reader as much usable information as possible for the time they spend. There is almost always room to cut. |
| 5 | Precision | Am I saying exactly what I mean? First decide what you mean, then choose the words that say it, and nothing more. |
| 6 | Context | Why does this matter to these people? Say why, out loud. If you can't say why a part matters, give it less space or cut it. |
| 7 | No distractions | Is there any verbal, written or visual distraction? Explain every reference the audience might not know, or remove it. Only show a visual while you are talking about it. |
| 8 | Engaging | Is there a point where attention could drift? Find the weakest section and fix it. People you lose there may not come back. |
| 9 | Useful | Have I answered the questions people will actually have? Write those questions down and check each one off. |
| 10 | Clarity of purpose | Above all, what am I trying to explain? Every sentence should serve that one purpose. |

The central tension is fitting in enough detail, complexity and context while keeping the piece simple and short enough that people stay with it. The steps below are how to manage it.

---

## Know the audience: five questions

1. **Target**: who exactly am I talking to?
2. **Knowledge**: what do they already know about this, and what do they *want* to know?
3. **Tailoring**: how do they like to receive information? (Brisk and headline-first, detailed, visual, practical takeaways?)
4. **Make it personal**: how can I show this is for *them*? Speak to particular groups directly. Watch for the people most likely to decide "this isn't for me" and pitch to them.
5. **Credibility**: why should they believe me on this? Find the gap in your credibility and close it. Build evidence of credibility into the explanation itself: sources, relevant experience, fairness.

If you can't find these out, say what you're assuming and go with it.

## The Seven Steps

### Step 1: Set-up
Write short answers (a sentence or two each):
- **Purpose**: what am I explaining, in one sentence at most? If it won't fit, write a paragraph and boil it down.
- Who is it for? Do they all know about the same amount? What's their level of knowledge? What do they want from this?
- What specific questions must it answer? (Start the list now and keep adding to it.)
- How do they like information delivered?
- Where will it be read, heard or watched, and in what format?
- How long can it be (words or minutes), and how strict is that limit?
- Can I find out more, for example by asking the organiser or reader? If so, do.

### Step 2: Find the information
- Put everything that might be relevant into one place, an "info dump". Don't worry about order yet. If you're unsure whether something belongs, include it. Cutting comes later.
- List the parts of the subject you think you need to cover.
- **Sources**: build the factual base from direct sources (the original) and reliable ones (intermediaries with a good track record). Note where every fact came from. Check the context of any quote. Vet extra sources for agenda and track record, and check whether other sources agree.
- Three habits: *What's the source?* · *Really?* (if something doesn't add up, it may be wrong, and the audience will stumble on it too) · *Who can I ask?*
- Keep three running lists: **audience questions**, **things I don't understand yet**, **areas of the subject to cover**. Close the gaps before going on.

### Step 3: Distil the information
- Keep the purpose in front of you. The test is *relevant and essential*, not *interesting*.
- Go through line by line. If something isn't relevant, move it to a parking area (bottom of the document or a separate file). If it's yes or maybe, keep it.
- Cut each kept item down to its nugget: the fact, figure, quote or argument, written as a fragment. Drop the sentence around it.
  - *Example:* "The council voted on Tuesday night, after a long debate, to cut the library budget by 30%, which will close two branches." → `Library budget cut 30%` · `Two branches close`
- Go through a second time. It will be faster, and you'll understand the subject better.
- For each element, ask: does it serve the purpose? Why does it matter? Every element has to do a job. If it doesn't, it's a distraction.
- Check for gaps. If you find any, repeat Steps 2–3 for that area.

### Step 4: Organise the information
**Strands.** Split the material into strands, the main sections, each with a short header. Roughly: 3 for a short meeting, about 5 for a ten-minute talk, 5–10 for a long essay or speech. Add two more:
- `NOT SURE`: material you haven't placed yet
- `HIGH IMPACT`: striking facts, quotes or images to use at the start and end

**Story.** (If the piece is a business recommendation, build it as a pyramid with `pyramid-principle` instead, then come back here for Steps 5–7.) Information alone rarely holds attention. A story makes people want to know what happens next. Decide:
- **Purpose**: what the audience should understand by the end.
- **Story**: the journey that gets them there.
- **How you'll tell it**: the structure.
- Five-word test: can you say what the story is in about five words, before you write and again after?
- To find the story: imagine a friend asks "What are you working on?" and answer out loud. Try opening with each strand in turn; most won't work, one or two will.
- Story structures to try:
  - **Chronological**: key developments in order.
  - **Outcome first**: start with the result, go back to the beginning, then work forward to the result again.
  - **Zoom out**: start with the specific event, then widen the context step by step.
  - **"You can't understand this without…"**: state the issue, then bring in each strand it depends on, one at a time.
  - **Anchor statement**: build the piece around one key finding or phrase and keep coming back to it.
  - **Problem → solution**: set out the problem, then how it was tackled, section by section.
  - **Build it in front of them**: each piece depends on the one before, so the audience watches the explanation assemble.
- If you're stuck, open by saying what the story is: "This is a story of…", "Today I'll take you through…", "Here are ten minutes on…".

**Order.** Put the strands in order. Then within each strand:
- What should this strand achieve? Which elements matter most? Which comes first, and what follows from what?
- Place elements one at a time. Know what job each one does. You don't have to use everything.
- Move leftovers to `NOT SURE`. If you notice a missing piece, write it down as a "shopping list item" and go and get it.
- Pick elements for an **Introduction** strand and an **End** strand.
- **Visuals**: use only specific, relevant images, charts, quotes or maps, never generic "wallpaper" (a talk about one particular car shows that car, not "cars"). Decide what's on screen at the start and what's left showing at the end.

### Step 5: Link the information (write the draft)
Start writing even before you feel ready, and redraft as many times as it takes. Write the way you would speak.
- **Trails** at the big junctions keep people with you: "We did three things to get there, and I'll show you each one."
- **Show the structure**: "Now we've looked at X, the next part is Y." · "X and Y matter, but neither matters as much as Z."
- **Signpost tangents** on the way out and on the way back: "Before we go on, we need two minutes on X." · "With that in mind, back to Y."
- **Joining phrases** at every link, not just the big junctions. They look back and forward at once, so there's never a dead stop: "That explains the drop. It doesn't explain why it happened so fast."
- **Hooks and back-references**: pick out a key word, figure or phrase from the element you just gave, repeat it, and use it to add emphasis or context and move on. ("Revenue fell 40%. Forty per cent, in one year, while competitors grew.") It's powerful, so use it sparingly.
- **Parallel strands**: switch between them with *while*, *as* and *meanwhile* to keep momentum.
- **Short sentences**: about 15 words maximum for anything spoken. If a sentence holds two ideas, split it.
- **Subject near the front**: don't make people wait through a list before they find out who or what it's about.
- **Beginning and end**: the opening hooks the audience and promises the story. The ending lands a clear conclusion and ideally echoes the opening.
- Read the whole draft top to bottom. Does it flow? Are there gaps? Did it stick to the story structure? Does it have momentum? Does it sound like a person?

### Step 6: Tighten
Go over the draft several times, asking:
1. **Obstacles to understanding**: Which names are really needed? Which dates (does it need the full date or just the year)? Which statistics (are several making the same point)? Is there any non-essential information?
2. **Unneeded complication**: is anything said in a harder way than necessary?
3. **Shorter sentences**: can a sentence lose words without losing meaning? Cut redundant pairs like *(all-time) record*, *gather (together)*, *postpone (until later)*, *during (the course of)*.
4. **Unexplained references**: sort words into four groups: support words, known words, partly known words, unknown words. For the last two, explain them or cut them. "As some of you will know…" covers people who already know without losing those who don't. If a reference is needed, explain it, even if that takes a sentence.
5. **Visuals match words**: does what's on screen match what's being said at that moment, without giving away what comes next?
6. **Start and finish**: does the opening draw people in and the ending give a clear conclusion? Do they work together?
7. **Questions answered**: go through the audience-questions list one by one.
8. **The big one**: is every strand and every element essential?

If you can, show it to someone else. Then check it's still enjoyable to read. A tight explanation still needs to be a pleasure to follow.

### Step 7: Delivery
- **Read it aloud.** Does it sound like you? Does each sentence lead into the next? If a word trips you up, rewrite it.
- **Written pieces**: use clear headers, short paragraphs and restrained emphasis, and read it as the reader would.
- **Spoken pieces**:
  - Use a large font, or bullet-point prompts made of trigger words. Cut the prompts down as far as you can while still remembering what to say.
  - Mark the script for emphasis and pauses.
  - Show each visual only when you reach it.
  - Check the timing.
  - Plan where you'll stand and what to do with your hands.
  - Rehearse each section, then the whole piece, ideally somewhere like the real venue. Record it if it really matters.

## How to apply this skill

**When producing an explanation for the user:**
- Do the Set-up quietly. If purpose, audience or length is unclear and the piece is substantial, ask one short question. Otherwise state your assumption in one line.
- Deliver the finished explanation, not the scaffolding. Show strands, story choice or tightening notes only if the user asks or it helps them.
- Before handing it over, run the ten tests and the Step 6 sweep.

**When reviewing or coaching the user's own draft:**
- Start with purpose: say back the purpose you see in it, in one sentence, and ask whether that's what they meant.
- Go through the ten tests and point to specific lines: what to cut, what to explain, where attention drops, which questions go unanswered.
- Suggest a story structure and an opening and ending if those are weak.
- Show tightening as before → after examples, not general advice.
