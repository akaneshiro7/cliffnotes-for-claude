# High-stakes conversations: staying in dialogue

Based on *Crucial Conversations: Tools for Talking When Stakes Are High* by Patterson, Grenny, McMillan and Switzler (2nd ed., 2012).

A conversation becomes **crucial** when three things are true: **the stakes are high, opinions differ, and emotions are strong**. Ironically, these are the conversations we handle worst: we either go quiet or come on too strong. Use this skill for: disagreeing with a senior person, pushing back on a deadline or a workload, giving or receiving tough feedback, raising a mistake or an ethical concern, conflict with a teammate, salary or performance talks, and difficult personal conversations.

**Scope.** This skill owns the emotional and interpersonal side: safety, motives, stories and listening. Other skills handle the rest:
- `pyramid-principle`: the logic of what you're proposing.
- `dynamic-explanation`: preparing the content when you'll face lots of questions.
- `quick-explanations`: if the conversation happens over email or chat, for the message mechanics.

---

## The goal: fill the pool of shared meaning

Everyone brings their own facts, opinions, feelings and theories. **Dialogue** is the free flow of meaning between people. The more relevant meaning gets into a *shared pool*, the better the decision and the more committed everyone is to it. The two failure modes both drain the pool:

| **Silence** (holding meaning back) | **Violence** (forcing meaning in) |
|---|---|
| *Masking*: understating, sarcasm, sugar-coating | *Controlling*: forcing your view, monopolising, cutting people off, overstating |
| *Avoiding*: steering away from the real issue | *Labelling*: putting people in boxes ("typical back office") |
| *Withdrawing*: pulling out of the conversation | *Attacking*: belittling, threatening |

Know your own **style under stress**: which of these you tend toward when things heat up.

## The seven skills

### 1. Start with heart: what do I really want?
Work on yourself first, because you're the only person you can directly control.
- When you notice yourself drifting toward silence or violence, ask: **what does my behaviour say about what I'm after right now?** (Winning? Being right? Punishing? Saving face? Keeping the peace?)
- Then ask: **What do I really want for myself? For them? For the relationship? And how would I act if I really wanted that?**
- **Refuse the fool's choice.** We often tell ourselves we must choose between being honest and keeping the relationship, or between speaking up and keeping our job. Look for the *and*: "How can I tell my VP the timeline is unrealistic **and** keep his trust **and** still deliver something useful by Friday?"

### 2. Learn to look: notice when it turns crucial
Watch the **conditions**, not just the content:
- the moment a conversation turns crucial (your body usually knows first: tight stomach, raised voice, a rush to reply);
- signs that **safety** is at risk, which show up as people moving to silence or violence;
- your own style under stress kicking in.

When you see it, step back from the content and attend to safety.

### 3. Make it safe
People don't get defensive because of *what* you say, but because they stop feeling safe. Safety rests on two conditions:
- **Mutual purpose**: they believe you care about their goals.
- **Mutual respect**: they believe you respect them.

When safety slips, step out of the content, restore safety, then step back in:
- **Apologise** when you've genuinely violated respect. ("I'm sorry I raised that in front of the whole team. That wasn't fair to you.")
- **Contrast** to fix a misunderstanding: say what you *don't* mean, then what you *do* mean. ("I'm not saying the analysis is wrong. I think the conclusion is solid. I am concerned the timeline doesn't leave room to check the inputs.")
- **Create a mutual purpose** when you're truly at cross-purposes (**CRIB**):
  - **C**ommit to finding a shared purpose. Say out loud that you'll keep talking until you find something that works for both of you.
  - **R**ecognise the purpose behind the strategy. Ask what each of you actually wants underneath the positions.
  - **I**nvent a broader purpose that both can sign up to.
  - **B**rainstorm new strategies that serve it.

### 4. Master my stories: control your emotions by retelling the story
Emotions don't come straight from what others do. There's a step in between, the **path to action**:

> **See / hear** (facts) → **Tell a story** (interpret: what it means, why they did it) → **Feel** (emotion) → **Act**

We invent the story in a split second and then treat it as fact. Change the story and the emotion changes.
- **Retrace your path**, working backwards: What am I doing (silence or violence)? What am I feeling? What story is creating that feeling? What are the actual **facts**, the things you could see or hear, behind it?
- **Separate facts from stories.** "He replied-all with corrections to my deck" is a fact. "He wanted to embarrass me" is a story.
- **Watch for three "clever" stories** that justify bad behaviour:
  - **Victim**: "It's not my fault." (Ignores your part.)
  - **Villain**: "It's their fault, because they're incompetent or malicious." (Ignores their reasonable side.)
  - **Helpless**: "There's nothing I can do." (Ignores your options.)
- **Tell the rest of the story** by asking:
  - Am I pretending not to notice my own role?
  - Why would a reasonable, rational, decent person do this?
  - What do I really want?
  - What would I do right now if I really wanted that?

### 5. STATE my path: speak persuasively, not abrasively
When you have a hard message, or feel so sure you might push too hard:
- **S**hare your facts: start with the least controversial, most persuasive facts. ("The model's due at 9am, and the data from the client arrived at 7pm.")
- **T**ell your story: the conclusion you're starting to draw. ("I'm worried that if we rush it, we'll send numbers we can't stand behind.")
- **A**sk for others' paths: invite their facts and story. ("Am I missing something about how hard that deadline is?")
- **T**alk tentatively: present your story as a story, not a fact. ("I'm starting to think…", "It seems to me…", "Perhaps…"). Tentative, not wishy-washy.
- **E**ncourage testing: make it safe to disagree. ("If you see it differently, I really want to hear it.") Mean it.

Facts first, because they're the most persuasive and least insulting part. The story comes second, tentatively.

### 6. Explore others' paths: listen when they go silent or violent
Be genuinely curious about how a reasonable person reached their view. Use **AMPP** to retrace their path to action:
- **A**sk: invite them to share. ("What's your take?", "What am I not seeing?")
- **M**irror: name the emotion you're seeing, calmly. ("You say it's fine, but you sound frustrated.")
- **P**araphrase: restate their story in your own words to show you understood. ("So you think the late data is the real issue, and you're getting blamed for it.")
- **P**rime: if they're still holding back, offer your best guess at what they're thinking. ("Are you worried this will look bad in your review?")

When it's your turn to respond, use **ABC**:
- **A**gree where you do agree. Most arguments are about the 5% we disagree on.
- **B**uild: if they're right but have left something out, agree, then add. ("Yes, and…")
- **C**ompare: where you really differ, don't say they're wrong. Lay out both views side by side. ("I see it differently. Let me explain how.")

### 7. Move to action: turn talk into results
Good conversations fail afterwards because of unclear expectations or no follow-through.
- **Decide how to decide** up front:
  - *Command*: someone decides alone.
  - *Consult*: gather input, then one person or a few decide.
  - *Vote*: the majority, or an agreed percentage, decides.
  - *Consensus*: everyone agrees and supports the decision.
  - Make it explicit who decides and how, especially across levels of seniority.
- **Finish clearly**: agree **who** does **what** by **when**, and how you'll **follow up**. Write it down, then follow up and hold people to it, yourself included.

## Before a crucial conversation: prep sheet

1. **What do I really want?** For me, for them, for the relationship. What do I *not* want? Find the *and* that gets past the fool's choice.
2. **Facts vs story:** list the observable facts, then the story you're telling yourself, then an alternative story a reasonable person might tell.
3. **Their likely path:** what do they want, and what might they be feeling or worried about?
4. **Safety risks:** where might they hear disrespect or doubt your purpose? Prepare a contrasting statement for each.
5. **STATE opener:** two or three facts, your tentative story, and a question inviting their view.
6. **Mutual purpose:** one sentence describing what you both want.
7. **The ask:** what you'd like to happen next, and how the decision will be made.

---

## How to apply this skill

- **Preparing the user:** fill in the prep sheet with them, then draft a concrete opening (contrast + facts + tentative story + question), two or three likely reactions with responses (AMPP/ABC), and a closing that agrees who does what by when. Keep phrasing natural and suited to the setting (e.g. junior analyst to MD: respectful, brief, facts first).
- **In the moment / quick help:** if the user is upset, help them separate facts from story and rewrite the message so it's tentative and safe before they send or say it. Turn accusatory drafts into STATE form.
- **Reviewing a message or conversation:** point out silence or violence (quote it), safety breaks and clever stories, and show before → after rewrites.
- **Practice:** if the user wants to rehearse, play the other person realistically, then debrief using these skills.
- Not a substitute for HR, legal or compliance channels. If a concern involves harassment, safety or misconduct, mention the proper reporting route alongside the conversation advice.
